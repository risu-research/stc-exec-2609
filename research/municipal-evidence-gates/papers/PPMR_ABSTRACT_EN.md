# Working Title

**Who Receives Monitoring, and What Counts as Improvement? Auditing Selection Decisions and Performance Claims in New York City's Heat Sensor Program**

## Expression of Interest Abstract (approximately 315 words)

Public agencies increasingly use performance data to decide where to concentrate enforcement. Yet the same administrative records used to justify these decisions may be insufficient to explain who was selected or to measure whether additional monitoring improved public outcomes. We develop an evidence-gated audit that links decision reconstruction, unresolved administrative exceptions, and the identification requirements of performance claims. New York City's Heat Sensor Program offers a concrete test: the Department of Housing Preservation and Development selects 50 apartment buildings annually for mandatory temperature sensors and intensified oversight, using prior heating violations, tenant complaints, and legal exclusions.

We reconstruct the published 2024 and 2025 selections against official building lists and linked public housing records, and test the limits of explanation using a separately evaluated later cohort. Under current-record eligibility proxies, the reconstructed 50th-place violation score is nine in both years. In 2024, 51 buildings appear at or above that score, so a statutory tie priority becomes relevant. In 2025, exactly 50 appear at or above nine, yet one officially selected building has eight qualifying citations under the observed reconstruction while a nine-citation building remains unselected. Reclassifying the dates of the eight citations does not resolve this discrepancy. We distinguish this observable explanation gap from evidence of administrative error, because historical scoring inputs, some legal exclusions, and additional statutory selection criteria remain unavailable.

We next evaluate whether legally plausible unselected buildings support causal comparisons. Strict matching yields 31 selected buildings in each cohort, but large imbalances in preselection heating violations remain and independent earlier-season trend-equivalence tests are inconclusive. At the selection boundary, matching on the exact total violation score, borough, unit counts, complaint-unit share, and season-specific violation histories yields no fully comparable pair. Finally, we trace a measurement disconnect: municipal performance indicators count attempted proactive visits, while a separate oversight report describes changes in tenant complaints. Neither by itself identifies changes in indoor temperature or the causal effect of enforcement.

The resulting audit specifies which administrative decisions are reproducible, which exceptions require additional records, and which equity and effectiveness claims the available evidence can support. It offers a practical protocol for performance systems where selection, monitoring intensity, and measured outcomes interact.

**Keywords:** public performance management; administrative targeting; social equity; heat enforcement; evidence traceability; causal identification

## Drafting safeguards

This EOI abstract is a prospective scholarly draft, not a statement that the full historical selection process or a causal effect has been independently recovered. Its quantitative results are based on publicly collected contemporary-proxy audits, not frozen historical scoring ledgers. The existing 2025 holdout performance was tested separately but uses the same law and related data sources; the paper should make the development and evaluation conditions explicit. The working title and abstract can be modified after stronger direct historical records are recovered.
